\section*{Executive Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Executive Summary}

Discovering high-performance permanent magnets with reduced rare-earth content is important for electric vehicles, wind turbines, and industrial machinery. However, magnetic materials are challenging to model because different magnetic properties are governed by different physical mechanisms across atomic, crystallographic, and microstructural length scales. Some magnetic properties, such as the Curie temperature ($T_C$) and Néel temperature ($T_N$), correlate well with chemical composition. Other properties, such as coercivity ($H_c$) and maximum energy product ($(BH)_{\max}$), depend strongly on crystal symmetry, local anisotropy, and microstructure, making them difficult to predict from composition alone.

\textbf{MagMat-Discovery} is an open machine learning and screening platform developed to predict both intrinsic and extrinsic magnetic properties. The platform integrates:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Curated Experimental Data}: A standardized dataset of 51,394 experimental entries from the Non-Equilibrium Magnetic Materials Database (NEMAD), linked to crystallographic symmetries from the Materials Project.
    \item \textbf{Physics-Guided Descriptors}: Featurization encoding valence electron concentration, superexchange transfer factors, spin-orbit proxies, and single-ion anisotropy terms.
    \item \textbf{Learned Representations}: A stoichiometric graph transformer (\textbf{MagFormer}) and equivariant structural embeddings from MACE.
    \item \textbf{Physical Cascades}: Enforcing thermodynamic ceilings and micromagnetic relationships so that downstream hysteresis predictions remain physically consistent.
    \item \textbf{Calibrated Uncertainty Ranges}: Conformal prediction intervals that provide an expected 90\% coverage for every continuous prediction.
\end{itemize}

Across 8 magnetic target properties evaluated under leak-free grouped cross-validation, the platform achieves strong performance on magnetic phase classification (91.9\% accuracy) and transition temperatures ($R^2 = 0.82$ for $T_C$, $R^2 = 0.74$ for $T_N$). For extrinsic hysteresis properties ($H_c, K_1, (BH)_{\max}$), the models achieve moderate predictive performance ($R^2 = 0.46$--$0.62$), providing an effective ranking tool to filter promising candidates before detailed synthesis.

\vspace{0.5cm}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Magnetic Materials, Physics-Informed Machine Learning, Graph Transformers, Conformal Calibration, Coercivity, Permanent Magnets, Materials Screening.

\section{Project Background and Objectives}

\subsection{Motivation and Practical Needs}
Permanent magnets are critical components in modern clean energy and transportation systems, including electric vehicle traction motors, wind turbine generators, and precision actuators \cite{coey2010}. The strongest commercially available magnets rely heavily on critical rare-earth elements, primarily Neodymium (Nd), Dysprosium (Dy), and Samarium (Sm). These elements are vulnerable to supply disruptions, price volatility, and environmentally damaging mining practices.

Developing alternatives with lower rare-earth content---or eliminating rare earths entirely---requires materials that meet three criteria:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Elevated Curie temperature ($T_C > 400\,\text{K}$) to prevent thermal demagnetization during operation.
    \item Substantial saturation magnetization ($\mu_0 M_s > 1.0\,\text{T}$) to ensure strong flux output.
    \item High coercivity ($H_c > 800\,\text{kA/m}$) to resist opposing demagnetizing fields.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why Machine Learning for Magnetism is Hard}
Applying machine learning to magnetic materials presents specific challenges that distinguish it from standard property prediction tasks:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Some properties are harder to predict than others:} Chemical composition alone correlates well with Curie and Néel temperatures because magnetic exchange is largely set by local chemical bonds. In contrast, coercivity ($H_c$) and anisotropy ($K_1$) depend strongly on crystal symmetry, grain boundaries, and defect pinning. Composition-only models typically struggle on these extrinsic targets.
    \item \textbf{Standard data splits can produce misleading metrics:} Experimental databases frequently contain many measurements of the same parent material with minor dopant substitutions. Standard random splits place identical chemical formulas in both training and test sets. This creates artificial memorization and inflates validation scores, while failing on genuinely new chemistries.
    \item \textbf{Independent models can violate physical laws:} When target properties are predicted independently, statistical models can produce contradictory results. For example, a model might predict an energy product $(BH)_{\max}$ that exceeds the theoretical ceiling ($\frac{1}{4}\mu_0 M_s^2$), or predict non-zero coercivity for a non-magnetic compound.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Objectives of this Work}
MagMat-Discovery was built to address these challenges through four clear objectives:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Unified Multi-Target Modeling:} Simultaneously predict 8 magnetic properties covering magnetic phase, transition temperatures, and hysteresis behavior.
    \item \textbf{Leak-Free Grouped Evaluation:} Evaluate all models using 5-fold cross-validation grouped by reduced chemical formula, ensuring identical compositions do not appear in both train and test sets.
    \item \textbf{Physics-Enforced Cascades:} Connect intrinsic predictions ($K_1, M_s$) to extrinsic predictions ($H_c, (BH)_{\max}$) using micromagnetic relationships and thermodynamic bounds.
    \item \textbf{Calibrated Ranges and Interactive Screening:} Provide 90\% conformal uncertainty ranges for every prediction and package the pipeline into an accessible web platform.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Data Curation and Split Design}

\subsection{Experimental and Crystallographic Data Sources}
MagMat-Discovery brings together experimental measurements and crystallographic metadata:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{NEMAD Database:} 51,394 curated experimental records from the Non-Equilibrium Magnetic Materials Database \cite{nemad2025}. These provide measured values for phase, $T_C$, $T_N$, $M_s$, $K_1$, $H_c$, $(BH)_{\max}$, and $\theta_p$.
    \item \textbf{Materials Project:} High-throughput crystal structures and symmetry labels \cite{materials_project}, providing space group numbers (1--230), crystal systems, and densities.
    \item \textbf{Bilbao MAGNDATA:} Magnetic space group ground truths for magnetic symmetry validation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data Cleaning and Feature Sanitation}
Prior to model training, the raw datasets undergo systematic cleaning:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of Unmeasurable Features:} DFT-computed total energy, formation energy, and calculated magnetic moments are intentionally excluded from input features. Because these quantities are unavailable for uncharacterized materials, including them would leak the ground-state phase.
    \item \textbf{Formula Canonicalization:} Chemical formulas are parsed and normalized to coprime integer stoichiometries (\texttt{reduced\_formula}) using \texttt{pymatgen}.
    \item \textbf{Logarithmic Scaling for Wide-Span Targets:} Properties that span multiple orders of magnitude ($H_c, K_1, (BH)_{\max}$) are transformed using base-10 logarithmic or symmetric-logarithmic scaling. This stabilizes variance during model training.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Grouped Data Partitioning and Leakage Controls}
To evaluate model generalization honestly, cross-validation is performed using 5-fold \texttt{GroupKFold} grouped by \texttt{reduced\_formula}. 

This grouping guarantees that identical chemical formulas and pure polymorphs (such as different crystal phases of $\text{Fe}_3\text{Pt}$ or repeated entries of $\text{Nd}_2\text{Fe}_{14}\text{B}$) never appear in both training and test folds simultaneously. 

It is worth noting that chemically substituted variants with distinct reduced formulas (for example, $\text{Nd}_2\text{Fe}_{13}\text{CoB}$ versus $\text{Nd}_2\text{Fe}_{14}\text{B}$) are assigned to separate groups. This tests whether the models can generalize across neighboring chemical substitutions without memorizing the exact stoichiometry.

\subsection{Multi-Tier Physics Descriptors}
Features are structured into four physical tiers. The underlying mathematical formulations are provided in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_physics}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Tier 1 (Stoichiometry and Elements):} Elemental mass fractions, atomic numbers, covalent radii, and Pauling electronegativities.
    \item \textbf{Tier 2 (Electronic Structure and Exchange Physics):} Valence electron concentration (VEC) relative to the Slater-Pauling maximum ($\text{VEC} \approx 8.3$); superexchange factors that model whether non-magnetic anions promote ferromagnetic or antiferromagnetic alignment; and spin-orbit proxies that account for heavy-element interactions.
    \item \textbf{Tier 3 (Crystal Field and Anisotropy):} Crystal system indicators and Stevens operator crystal-field terms that signal whether an alloy favors an easy-axis ($K_1 > 0$) or easy-plane orientation.
    \item \textbf{Tier 4 (Structural Foundation Embeddings):} Pretrained representations from the MACE-MP-0 equivariant model \cite{mace2022} for compounds with known crystal coordinates.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Deep Representation Learning with MagFormer}
When screening hypothetical compositions, exact atomic coordinates are often unavailable. To extract learned chemical representations from formula strings alone, we developed \textbf{MagFormer}, a stoichiometric graph transformer.

MagFormer represents each chemical formula as a fully connected element graph. Constituents are initialized with elemental properties, and multi-head attention is modulated by pairwise property differences. Stoichiometry-weighted pooling produces a 384-dimensional representation vector for each compound. These vectors are passed as auxiliary features to the gradient-boosted ensembles. Detailed network specifications are provided in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_magformer}.

\subsection{Ensemble Regressors and Micromagnetic Cascades}
Each target property is modeled using an ensemble of five diverse regressors: CatBoost, LightGBM, XGBoost, ExtraTrees, and Random Forest. Predictions are combined using non-negative least squares meta-blending. For ground-state phase identification, a dedicated boundary classifier is trained specifically on ambiguous compounds where predicted FM and AFM probabilities are close ($|P(\text{FM}) - P(\text{AFM})| < 0.20$).

To prevent unphysical predictions in extrinsic hysteresis targets, the pipeline applies a physical cascade:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Intrinsic properties ($K_1$ and $M_s$) are predicted first.
    \item Their out-of-fold predictions are passed as inputs to the coercivity ($H_c$) and energy product ($(BH)_{\max}$) models. This informs the model of the Kronmüller coercivity relationship \cite{kronmuller2003}:
    \begin{equation}
        H_c \approx \alpha_{\text{micro}} \frac{2 K_1}{\mu_0 M_s} - N_{\text{eff}} M_s
    \end{equation}
    where $\alpha_{\text{micro}}$ reflects microstructural reduction and $N_{\text{eff}}$ is an effective demagnetizing factor.
    \item Predicted energy products are bounded by the thermodynamic ceiling: $(BH)_{\max} \le \frac{1}{4}\mu_0 M_s^2$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Calibrated Uncertainty Estimation}
Every continuous prediction is accompanied by a range. We apply split conformal prediction \cite{vovk2005} using out-of-fold validation residuals. The 90th percentile of the absolute residuals defines a calibration margin:
\begin{equation}
    q_{\text{conf}} = \text{Quantile}_{0.90} \left( \{ |y_i - \hat{y}_i| \}_{i=1}^n \right)
\end{equation}
For any new material, the prediction range is given by $[\hat{y} - q_{\text{conf}},\, \hat{y} + q_{\text{conf}}]$. Under standard exchangeability, approximately 90\% of true values fall within this range.

\section{Model Validation and Performance Evaluation}

\subsection{How to Read the Evaluation Metrics}
Before examining the benchmark results, it is helpful to clarify what each metric means:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$):} Measures the proportion of variance explained by the model. An $R^2$ of 1.0 represents perfect prediction, while an $R^2 \le 0$ indicates the model performs no better than guessing the dataset mean. Values above 0.70 represent strong predictive accuracy, while values between 0.40 and 0.65 represent moderate predictive utility suitable for candidate pre-screening and ranking.
    \item \textbf{Mean Absolute Error (MAE):} For linear properties ($T_C, T_N, M_s, \theta_p$), MAE is reported in physical units (Kelvin or emu/g). For logarithmic targets ($H_c, (BH)_{\max}$), MAE is reported in \textbf{dex} (orders of magnitude on a $\log_{10}$ scale). An MAE of 0.65 dex on coercivity means predictions are typically off by a multiplicative factor of $10^{0.65} \approx 4.5$, reflecting the extreme sensitivity of coercivity to microstructure.
    \item \textbf{Classification Metrics:} Magnetic phase classification is evaluated using overall accuracy and Macro $F_1$, which measures balanced performance across imbalanced classes.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary and Future Perspectives}
MagMat-Discovery demonstrates how physics-informed featurization, graph transformer representations, and micromagnetic cascades can be combined into an accessible materials screening platform. Evaluating models under leak-free grouped cross-validation provides an honest assessment of capabilities:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=1.5pt, parsep=0pt]

\section{Physical Formulations for Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:appendix_physics}

This appendix provides the formal equations for the physics-guided descriptors described in Section~3.2.

\subsection{Goodenough-Kanamori Superexchange Transfer Factors}
In transition metal oxides, sulfides, and pnictides, indirect exchange between magnetic cations ($M$) mediated by non-magnetic anions ($X$) follows Goodenough-Kanamori rules. The transfer factor models the competition between ferromagnetic direct exchange and antiferromagnetic superexchange:
\begin{equation}
    J_{\text{ex}} \propto \cos^2\theta \cdot \exp\left( - \frac{|\chi_{\text{cat}} - \chi_{\text{anion}}|}{\chi_0} \right)
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is the $M\text{--}X\text{--}M$ bond angle, $\chi_{\text{cat}}$ and $\chi_{\text{anion}}$ denote Pauling electronegativities, and $\chi_0$ is a characteristic scaling parameter ($\chi_0 \approx 1.5$). When $\theta \approx 180^\circ$, strong antiferromagnetic superexchange dominates; near $90^\circ$, ferromagnetic direct exchange is favored.

\subsection{Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya Interaction (DMI) Proxy}
In non-centrosymmetric space groups containing elements with strong spin-orbit coupling, the broken inversion symmetry introduces an antisymmetric exchange interaction (DMI) that induces chiral spin canting:
\begin{equation}
    D_{\text{proxy}} = \mathbb{I}(\text{non-centrosymmetric}) \sum_{i} x_i \left( \frac{Z_i}{100} \right)^4 \cdot \mathbb{I}(Z_i \ge 40)
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function, $x_i$ is the stoichiometric fraction of element $i$, and $Z_i$ is its atomic number. The fourth-power dependence reflects the relativistic scaling of spin-orbit coupling ($\lambda_{\text{SOC}} \propto Z^4$).

\subsection{Crystal Field and Stevens Operator Hamiltonian}
In uniaxial crystallographic systems (tetragonal, hexagonal, and trigonal), the first-order magnetocrystalline anisotropy constant $K_1$ arises primarily from single-ion crystal field interactions:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_{\text{CF}} = B_2^0 O_2^0 + B_4^0 O_4^0
\end{equation}
where $B_n^m$ are crystal-field parameters and $O_n^m$ are Stevens operator equivalents. The second-order operator is defined by:
\begin{equation}
    O_2^0 = 3 J_z^2 - J(J+1)
\end{equation}
where $J$ is the total angular momentum quantum number. The sign of the second-order Stevens factor $\alpha_J$ dictates whether the single-ion interaction favors an easy axis ($\alpha_J < 0$, uniaxial anisotropy $K_1 > 0$) or an easy plane ($\alpha_J > 0$).

\section{MagFormer Architecture and Pooling Details}
\label{sec:appendix_magformer}

This appendix provides the formal network equations for the MagFormer stoichiometric graph transformer described in Section~3.3.

\subsection{Node Initialization and Projection}
Each constituent element $i$ in a chemical formula is embedded through a combination of discrete atomic number embeddings and normalized elemental physical features:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}_i^{(0)} = \mathbf{W}_{\text{node}} \left[ \mathbf{e}_{Z_i} \;\parallel\; x_i \;\parallel\; \mathbf{p}_i \right]
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{e}_{Z_i} \in \mathbb{R}^{64}$ is a learned element embedding vector, $x_i \in (0, 1]$ is the stoichiometric fraction, and $\mathbf{p}_i$ is a normalized vector of elemental physical properties (electronegativity, covalent radius, valence, and Stoner parameter).

\subsection{Pair-Bias Self-Attention}
Constituent elements interact via multi-head self-attention modulated by a learned pairwise relational bias matrix $\mathbf{B}_{ij}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A}_{ij} = \text{softmax} \left( \frac{\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} + \mathbf{B}_{ij} \right) \mathbf{V}_j
\end{equation}
where query ($\mathbf{Q}$), key ($\mathbf{K}$), and value ($\mathbf{V}$) projections are computed from $\mathbf{h}_i^{(l)}$. The pairwise bias $\mathbf{B}_{ij}$ is generated by passing elemental feature differences $|\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j|$ through a two-layer multi-layer perceptron.

\subsection{Leakage-Free Composition Pooling}
To aggregate the multi-element representations into a fixed-length composition vector without leaking task-specific targets, MagFormer uses a composite pooling operation combining fraction-weighted sums, masked maximums, and global means:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_{\text{comp}} = \left[ \sum_{i=1}^N x_i \mathbf{h}_i^{(L)} \;\parallel\; \max_{i=1 \dots N} \mathbf{h}_i^{(L)} \;\parallel\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{h}_i^{(L)} \right] \in \mathbb{R}^{384}
\end{equation}
where $L$ is the final transformer layer and $N$ is the number of constituent elements. The resulting 384-dimensional representation encodes complex non-linear chemical interactions and is supplied as an input to the tabular ensemble models.
